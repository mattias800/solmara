Gemini Plate is a steel-plate reverb: a sheet of steel two meters by one, hung in a frame, shaken by a driver at one point and heard by two pickups at others. It is the bright, dense, smooth reverb of countless vocals, snares and string sections, with no room in it: a plate has no walls to reflect from, so its sound is dense from the first milliseconds and has no echoes you can pick out.

Gemini Plate is built as the sheet itself, tens of thousands of its resonances ringing together, not as a recording of one played back.

## How the sound is made

1. Both sides of the input are summed into one, as the original had one driver.
2. **Pre-delay** waits before the plate is struck.
3. The plate rings. A damping plate beside it, set by **Decay**, takes the lows away sooner or later; the highs ring as long as the steel lets them.
4. The two pickups hear the plate differently, which is where its stereo comes from. **Low cut** and **High cut** shape what they hear, and **Width** narrows them toward mono.
5. **Mix** sets how much of the plate is heard beside your sound, and **Output** the level of the whole.

## The controls

### Decay

How long the plate rings, in seconds, at 200 Hz: from half a second up to the 4.9 seconds the plate rings with its damper as far away as it goes. On the original this moved a damping plate closer to the steel, and it works the same here: it shortens the lows most and hardly changes the highs, so a short plate stays bright.

The picture on the face draws how long each frequency rings, from 50 Hz to 20 kHz. **Drag it up and down** to move the damper, as you would turn **Decay**. Its whole travel is shaded behind it, and the cuts gray out the frequencies they take away.

You can turn **Decay** while the plate rings: what rings goes on, at the new rate.

### Pre-delay

Up to 250 ms before the plate is struck. 20 to 40 ms keeps a vocal's words clear of the reverb; longer is an audible gap, for effect.

### Low cut and High cut

Two filters on the plate's sound only, never on your dry sound. **Low cut** (80 Hz as it starts) keeps the plate out of the bass; raise it to 200 to 400 Hz to keep the reverb on a vocal from muddying the mix. **High cut** takes the sizzle off: 6 to 8 kHz is a darker, older plate.

### Width

The pickups' stereo, from both of them at full width down to mono, where the plate's sound is one in the middle.

### Mix

How much of the plate you hear beside the sound as it came, 25% as it starts. On a send track for the reverb, set it to 100%, so the track gives only the plate.

### Output

The level of everything that leaves the device, from -24 to +24 dB.

### Quality

How many of the plate's resonances run. **Light**, as it starts, runs about a tenth of them and keeps the tail as dense and as long; **Middle** keeps the first strike's crispness up to 1.5 kHz; **Full** runs every one, and costs by far the most. The tails sound alike; the difference is in the very start of the sound, most of all on drums. Use Full where the plate is heard bare.

## Using it

- **As a send.** Put it on a send track with **Mix** at 100%, and send the tracks that should share the plate. One plate for a song's vocals and snare holds them together.
- **On a vocal.** Decay 1.8 to 2.5 s, Pre-delay 30 ms, Low cut 250 Hz, High cut 9 kHz.
- **On a snare.** Decay 1.2 to 1.6 s, no pre-delay, Low cut 150 Hz, Quality Full for its crack.
- **Long and lush.** Decay at the top, Pre-delay 60 ms, High cut 7 kHz, for strings, pads and ballads.

## Tips

- A plate makes no latency: it adds no delay of its own to the track.
- A plate's lows build up quickly in a mix. If a reverb sounds muddy, raise **Low cut** before you shorten **Decay**.
- The tail carries on across your edits while the song plays.
