**An emulation of an analog original,** modeled on the early-1980s Japanese six-voice polysynth with one oscillator a voice and a stereo chorus.

[June Bloom on the website](/solmara/instruments/june-bloom/)

June Bloom is a six-voice polyphonic synthesizer in the image of the Japanese polysynths of the early and mid-1980s: one oscillator a voice, one resonant low-pass filter, one envelope, one LFO, and a bucket-brigade stereo chorus that turns all of that into something enormous. Those instruments were affordable, simple and stable, and they ended up everywhere: the pads of eighties pop, the stabs and basses of early house, the warm chords of synthwave today.

The design is a lesson in doing a lot with a little. There is no second oscillator to detune, so the width comes from the chorus; there is one envelope, so the filter and the amplifier move together; and the oscillator is digitally controlled, so it stays in tune all evening. June Bloom models each of those stages from its circuit: the oscillator's counter and ramp, the filter's four transconductance stages, the envelope's curves and the chorus's two delay lines.

## At a glance

- **Six voices**, each with its own oscillator, filter, amplifier and envelope.
- **One digitally controlled oscillator (DCO)** a voice: saw and pulse, each switched on or off, a square sub-oscillator an octave down, and white noise.
- **Pulse-width modulation** by hand or by the LFO.
- **A 24 dB/octave low-pass filter (VCF)** that self-oscillates, with envelope (either polarity), LFO and keyboard tracking.
- **A four-position high-pass filter (HPF)** after the voices, from a bass lift to a thin cut.
- **One ADSR envelope** a voice, for the filter and the amplifier, or the amplifier opened by the key alone (gate).
- **One triangle LFO** with a delay, shared by all six voices.
- **A stereo bucket-brigade chorus** with modes I, II and I+II.
- **Two voice-assignment modes**, glide, and a bender for the pitch, the filter and the LFO, with the mod wheel bringing in the LFO.

Read it from the top left. The DCO makes the raw sound: a mix of its saw, its pulse, its sub-oscillator and noise. The VCF takes away the highs above its cutoff, moved by the envelope, the LFO, the key you play and the bender. The VCA sets the voice's loudness by the same envelope, or by the key alone. The six voices are then added together and go through the high-pass, the chorus and the volume.

The gold lines are modulation: what moves what. The LFO reaches the pitch and the pulse width (both in the DCO) and the cutoff; the envelope reaches the cutoff and the amplifier.

## The oscillator: DCO

An analog oscillator drifts with temperature. June Bloom's, like its original's, does not: a digital counter divides a fast master clock by a whole number for each note, and every time the count runs out, an analog ramp is reset. The ramp is the saw; the pitch is exactly as stable as the clock. The price is that the highest notes can only land on the nearest whole divisor, so they are a few cents off, as on the original.

The other waves are made from the ramp:

- **Saw.** The ramp itself: bright, buzzy, every harmonic present. The starting point of strings, brass and most pads.
- **Pulse.** The ramp compared against a level: high while the ramp is under it, low above it. **Pulse width** (PWM) sets the level. At 5 it is a square, hollow and woody; toward the ends it narrows to a thin, nasal pulse.
- **Sub-oscillator** (SUB). A square an octave down, made by a flip-flop that changes state at every reset. It does not add brightness, only weight: the body under a bass or a pad.
- **Noise.** White noise, one source for all six voices, for breath, wind, surf and percussion.

**Saw** and **Pulse** are switches, as on the original; the sub and the noise are levels. With both waves on, the voice is brighter and fuller than either alone.

**Range** sets the octave: 16', 8' or 4', the organ-pipe lengths that synthesizers borrowed.

### Moving the pulse width

**Pulse width by** chooses who sets the width. On **MAN** (manual) the **Pulse width** slider sets it. On **LFO**, the slider sets how far the LFO sweeps it. A slowly swept pulse is the sound of the era: the harmonics shift continuously, and a single oscillator sounds like several slightly detuned ones. Try LFO rate 3 and Pulse width 5 with the pulse alone and the chorus off, then turn the chorus on.

### Vibrato

**LFO to pitch** (LFO, in the DCO section) sends the LFO to the pitch. 1 to 2 is vibrato; at the top it is a siren. Combine it with the LFO's delay for vibrato that arrives after the note starts.

The oscillators run freely: a key does not restart them, so no two notes start quite alike. That is part of the warmth.

## The high-pass filter: HPF

After the six voices are summed, a simple high-pass with four positions (FREQ):

| Position | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **0** | Lifts the bass: a low shelf, for a rounder, heavier sound. |
| **1** | Flat: nothing taken, nothing added. A new June Bloom starts here. |
| **2** | Takes away the bass under about 240 Hz. |
| **3** | Takes away the bass under about 770 Hz: thin and bright. |

It is a mixing tool built into the synth. A pad on 2 or 3 leaves room for the bass and the kick; a bass on 0 gains weight.

## The low-pass filter: VCF

This is where June Bloom's sound is shaped. It is a four-pole low-pass filter, falling at 24 dB an octave above its cutoff, built like the original's from four transconductance stages in a row with a feedback loop round them. Each stage bends gently when driven hard, which is why the filter sounds smooth rather than glassy.

- **Cutoff** (FREQ) sets where the highs start to go. Its scale is exponential: about 69 Hz at 2.5, 860 Hz at 5 and 10 kHz at 7.5.
- **Resonance** (RES) feeds the filter's output back to its input, which boosts the frequencies at the cutoff. A little gives the sound a vocal edge; from about 8 the filter rings by itself, and at 10 it sings a sine wave of its own. As with most filters of this kind, more resonance means less bass.
- **Envelope to filter** (ENV) is how far the envelope moves the cutoff, up to 14 octaves at 10. It is the control that makes a sound pluck, swell or bark.
- **Envelope polarity** (+ENV and -ENV): on negative, the envelope closes the filter as it rises, so a sound gets darker on its attack and brighter as it fades.
- **LFO to filter** (LFO) sweeps the cutoff with the LFO, up to about 3.3 octaves: a slow breathing, or a fast wah.
- **Key follow** (KYBD) opens the filter as you play higher. At 10 the cutoff follows the keyboard an octave an octave, so the sound is equally bright across the keys and a self-oscillating filter plays in tune; at 0, high notes are darker than low ones.

The bender can move the cutoff too; see [Performance](#performance).

## The amplifier and envelope: VCA and ENV

There is one envelope for each voice, and it does two jobs: it opens the filter (by **Envelope to filter**) and it shapes the loudness.

- **Attack** is the time to rise to full, from 3 ms to about 3 s, in a straight line.
- **Decay** is the time to fall to the sustain level, from 4 ms to 15 s.
- **Sustain** is the level held while the key is down.
- **Release** is the time to fade after the key is let go, from under 1 ms to about 14 s.

Decay and release fall as curves, quickly at first and then slowly, like a capacitor discharging. That is why a June Bloom pluck has a snap to it.

**Amplifier by** (ENV and GATE) chooses what opens the amplifier. On **Envelope**, the envelope shapes the loudness. On **Gate**, the amplifier opens fully while the key is held and closes when it is let go, like an organ, while the envelope still moves the filter. Gate lets you have a filter sweep on a sound that does not fade.

**Amplifier level** (LEVEL) sets the level of each voice, about 10 dB either way of the middle.

## The LFO

One LFO serves all six voices, as on the original. Its wave is a triangle, made in small steps, and it runs from a slow 0.04 Hz (one cycle in 25 seconds) to 30 Hz.

**LFO delay** (DELAY) is the clever part. When you strike a key with no others held, the LFO waits (from 0.2 s up to 3.3 s along the slider) and then grows in. A long note starts plain and gains its vibrato or filter movement the way a string player's or a singer's would. Keys played while others are held do not restart the delay.

Because the LFO is shared, a vibrato or a filter sweep moves the whole chord together. That is a large part of the instrument's character: a chord breathes as one.

## The chorus

The chorus is half of June Bloom's sound. It is a bucket-brigade chorus: two analog delay lines, each a chain of capacitors that pass the sound along at the speed of a clock. A slow triangle sweeps the clock, so the delay grows and shrinks, which bends the pitch of the delayed sound slightly up and down. The two lines are swept in opposite directions, one to each side, and mixed with the dry sound, so a mono voice comes out wide and shimmering.

| Mode | Speed | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **I** | 0.41 Hz | Slow and wide: the classic pad. |
| **II** | 0.75 Hz | Nearly twice as fast, the same depth: more obvious movement. |
| **I+II** | 8 Hz | A fast, shallow shimmer, the same in both sides: an organ's flutter. |

**Chorus output** (MONO and ST) plays it in stereo or folds it to mono. A bucket-brigade line also adds a little noise of its own and softens the highs, which June Bloom keeps: the chorus sounds like hardware, not like a clean digital effect.

To hear what it does, play a chord with the chorus off, then on I.

## Performance

These controls are in the window's **MORE** strip.

- **Voices** (POLY 1 and POLY 2). In **Poly 1**, each new key takes the next voice in turn, so a released note rings on under the next one: chords and pads blend. In **Poly 2**, a key takes the voice let go last, cutting its release: lines and basses stay tight.
- **Glide** slides each voice from its last pitch to its new key, at a steady rate: about 0.06 s an octave at 2, 0.27 s at 4, 0.86 s at 6, 1.6 s at 8 and 12.5 s at 10. The original had no glide; June Bloom adds it. In Poly 2 each note glides from the one before, which is the setting for a gliding bass line.
- **Bender** is the pitch-bend lever, -1 to 1, and the track's pitch bend moves it. **Bender to pitch** (TO PITCH) sets the range, an octave at 10. **Bender to filter** (TO FILTER) moves the cutoff, and **Bender to LFO** (TO LFO) sets how much LFO the lever's push brings in.
- **Bender's push to LFO** (PUSH) brings in the LFO by hand, 0 to 1; your keyboard's **mod wheel** pushes it too. Put some **LFO to pitch** in, leave the LFO's delay at 0, and the mod wheel adds vibrato as you play.
- **Volume** is the output level.
- **Quality** (Full or Light): Full runs each voice's filter at twice the sample rate, which keeps a high, resonant filter clean. Light runs it at the sample rate and costs half the processing; it sounds nearly the same.

Six notes sound at once; with six held, a seventh is not played. June Bloom does not answer velocity, as the original did not: the dynamics come from the filter and the envelope.

## Your first patch: a warm pad

Start from a new June Bloom and build a pad step by step, listening after each step.

1. **Make it slow.** Attack 5, Decay 6, Sustain 8, Release 6. Each chord now swells in and fades out.
2. **Make it dark.** Cutoff 4, Resonance 1, Envelope to filter 2, so the swell opens the filter a little.
3. **Make it move.** Switch the pulse on, set **Pulse width by** to LFO, Pulse width 4 and LFO rate 3. The tone starts to shift slowly.
4. **Make it wide.** Chorus II.
5. **Make room in the mix.** HPF 2.

Now change one thing at a time to hear what it does: the chorus between I and II, the saw on and off, a little Noise for air.

## Starting points

- **Round bass.** Range 16', Sub-oscillator 8, Cutoff 3, Resonance 3, Envelope to filter 5, Attack 0, Decay 3.5, Sustain 2, Release 1.5. Chorus off, Voices Poly 2, HPF 0.
- **House organ stab.** Amplifier by Gate, Pulse on, Saw off, Pulse width 5, Cutoff 7, Chorus I+II. Play short chords.
- **Brass.** Saw only, Cutoff 3.5, Envelope to filter 6, Attack 1.5, Decay 4, Sustain 5, Release 2. Chorus I.
- **Pluck.** Cutoff 2.5, Envelope to filter 7, Key follow 7, Attack 0, Decay 3, Sustain 0, Release 3. Chorus II.
- **Strings with late vibrato.** Saw, Cutoff 5, Attack 4, Release 5, LFO to pitch 1.5, LFO rate 5.5, LFO delay 5, Chorus I.
- **Singing filter.** Saw, Pulse and Sub-oscillator off, Resonance 10, Key follow 10, Envelope to filter 0: the filter's own sine is the sound, played in tune.
- **Gliding lead.** Voices Poly 2, Glide 3, Saw, Sub-oscillator 3, Cutoff 6, Resonance 3, Chorus off.

## Tips

- **Try the chorus first.** Before reaching for another effect, switch between I and II; most of June Bloom's sounds are built around one of them.
- **One envelope does two jobs.** For a pad that sweeps without fading, set Amplifier by to Gate and let the envelope work the filter alone.
- **For a fatter sound, use the sub before the resonance.** Resonance thins the bass; the sub-oscillator adds it back.
- **Six voices are a real limit.** A long release on a fast part steals voices; shorten the release, or use Poly 2.
- **For two layers**, put a second June Bloom on another track, or both in an [instrument rack](../instrument-rack/), one an octave apart by its Range.
- **June Bloom's chorus is also an effect of its own**, the [June Chorus](../../effects/june-chorus/), for any track.
